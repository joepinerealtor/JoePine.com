Step 1

Congratulations on taking the first step toward homeownership.

You may not know yet whether buying is the right move for you right now. This first step is about understanding what homeownership can solve for you, what responsibility comes with it, and why it can become a long-term wealth-building tool instead of just another monthly bill.

This is a real first step

Getting curious about buying matters. You do not need perfect timing or perfect finances yet. You are starting to see whether ownership could become a better fit for your goals, lifestyle, and timeline.

What ownership can solve

For some people, homeownership solves for stability, control over the space, room to stay longer, and the chance to stop putting every housing dollar toward someone else's property.

How wealth can start to build

When you own, part of your payment may reduce your loan balance, and if the home's value rises over time, that gap can become equity you are building for yourself.

Pros to know

  • You may get more control over pets, projects, design choices, and how long you stay.
  • A fixed-rate loan can create more stability in the principal-and-interest part of the payment than rent usually gives.
  • Part of your payment may build equity instead of only building a rent history.
  • That equity can matter later if you refinance, sell, or buy your next home.

Tradeoffs to respect

  • Repairs, maintenance, and house surprises become your responsibility.
  • Buying takes cash, planning, and a monthly payment that actually fits real life.
  • Owning is less flexible if you may need to move again very soon.
  • Homeownership works best when it solves something meaningful for you, not just because it sounds like the "next thing" to do.
The five-year picture later in this workbook will show the numbers more clearly, but the basic idea is this: renters build a payment history, while owners may build equity through principal paydown and appreciation over time.

What to notice before the numbers

  • Homeownership can solve for stability, control, and long-term wealth building, but it also comes with more responsibility.
  • You do not have to decide everything today. This step is about understanding what homeownership could do for you and what it would ask of you.
  • The next steps will show how five-year cost, monthly payment, and equity can shape the bigger picture.

Step 4

Now build the order of your plan.

The first few steps are there to help you see why homeownership can matter. From here, the workbook shifts into the same order many buyers usually experience: learn the language, clean up the file, understand payment and cash, get preapproved, then move into search, offer, and closing.

You can start before you are fully ready

Most buyers do not begin with perfect credit, perfect timing, or every dollar in place. They begin by understanding what will move them forward.

Small improvements can change the timing

Credit cleanup, lower monthly debt, and better cash planning can make buying feel a lot more possible than it did a few months earlier.

This is where the workbook gets practical

From here, the goal is simple: understand what to work on first, what comes next, and how the process usually unfolds once you are ready to move.

What to do next

  • Keep the homeowner goal in view, even if your timing is still coming together.
  • Use the next steps in order: buyer terms, credit, loan path, real payment, assistance, cash readiness, and preapproval.
  • After that, the workbook will shift naturally into what search, offer, and contract-to-close usually look like.

Joe would be happy to sit down with you and help turn this into a real plan. The goal is clarity and confidence, so you can understand what would move you from renter to homeowner in a way that actually makes sense for your life.

Step 5

Learn the real estate language before you hear it in conversation.

A lot of real estate words get said like everybody already knows them. This step is here so escrow, appraisal, title, deed, mortgage insurance, and down payment assistance feel more familiar before you are hearing them from lenders, agents, attorneys, and inspectors.

Money terms

  • Escrow: money collected and held to pay something later, often taxes and insurance, or sometimes deposits in the contract.
  • Mortgage insurance: an added cost on some lower-down-payment loans that protects the lender, not the homeowner.
  • DPA / down payment assistance: program money or secondary financing that may help with the down payment and sometimes closing costs.

Property check terms

  • Inspection: your chance to understand the home's condition, systems, repairs, and risks before you fully commit.
  • Appraisal: the lender's value check to make sure the home supports the price and loan amount.
  • Earnest money: the deposit that shows you are serious once you are under contract, usually credited back toward closing.

Ownership terms

  • Title: the legal ownership history and rights tied to the property.
  • Deed: the document that actually transfers ownership from seller to buyer.
  • Closing: the final signing and money-transfer stage where the purchase becomes official.

How to use this step

  • You do not need to memorize every term today. The goal is to make the language feel less intimidating when it comes up later.
  • If someone says a word you do not know, that is normal. Ask them to slow down and define it in plain English.
  • The next steps will bring these terms back in context so they start to feel familiar instead of technical.

Step 6

Know what is actually on your credit.

Most people do not need to guess their way into buying a home. Start by seeing the real picture: payment history, credit-card balances, collections, and monthly debt.

Pull the real reports

Start with the actual bureau data, not just a score from a phone app.

Find what is fixable

Late payments, high card balances, and unresolved collections usually matter more than people think.

Get specific

The goal is not just a higher score. The goal is knowing which move helps your buying path most.

What to do next

  • Pull your credit reports and make sure the accounts, balances, and late payments actually look right.
  • List every monthly debt payment, including cars, student loans, credit cards, and personal loans.
  • Flag anything that looks wrong, duplicate, or too old before you ever start a preapproval conversation.

Joe can help you turn this into an actual plan. Sometimes the next best move is credit cleanup. Sometimes it is just understanding payment comfort before you talk to a lender.

Step 7

You do not need perfect credit to buy a home.

Think of these as common starting points, not promises. Lenders can add their own overlays, but this is still a useful way to understand which credit range may be worth exploring first.

Many conventional paths use 620+ as a practical starting point. That does not mean every 620 file works, but it is a common benchmark.

Common starting point Often 620+
What else matters Debt load, cash reserves, and clean recent history
A 620 score with manageable debt and stable cash can be more workable than a higher score with heavy monthly obligations.

FHA is often where people start when they need more flexibility. A common starting point is 580+ for 3.5% down, while 500-579 may still be possible with 10% down.

What to do next

  • Figure out which credit starting point looks most like you right now.
  • Assume the lender may still be stricter than the headline minimum.
  • Use this step to decide whether your next move is credit cleanup, cash cleanup, or a lender conversation.

Step 8

Understand the real monthly payment before you add it.

A lot of buyers are closer than they think once they understand the real monthly picture. It also helps to know that the monthly payment is usually more than just principal and interest. Taxes, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance when it applies, and sometimes HOA dues all affect the real number.

What the payment usually includes

When most buyers say "mortgage payment," they are usually talking about the full monthly housing number, not just the loan itself.

  • Principal and interest: the core loan payment that pays back the balance and interest to the lender.
  • Mortgage insurance: often shows up on lower-down-payment loans and depends on the loan type and structure.
  • Property taxes: usually collected monthly through escrow even though the tax bill itself is not monthly.
  • Homeowners insurance: protects the home and is often built into the monthly payment through escrow too.
  • HOA dues, if applicable: not part of the mortgage note, but still part of the real monthly housing cost.

What homeownership adds

Owning can create stability and wealth over time, but more of the responsibility moves onto you once the home is yours.

  • Repairs and maintenance are no longer the landlord's job.
  • Utilities can look different from renting, depending on the home: water, sewer, trash, oil, gas, electric, or condo fees.
  • Some months are predictable, and some bring an unexpected repair or seasonal expense.
  • It helps to keep a house buffer for repairs, routine upkeep, and seasonal expenses.
Two homes at the same price can still have very different real monthly costs because taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance, and HOA dues can all change the total.

Pay down card balances

Keeping card balances lower helps more than many buyers realize. Lower utilization can support a stronger score and a cleaner monthly picture.

Avoid new monthly payments

Cars, personal loans, furniture financing, and store cards can all shrink what a lender is willing to approve.

Tighten recurring expenses

If your monthly budget already feels stretched, it is worth going line by line before you add homeownership costs on top.

What to do next

  • Try to keep credit-card balances low relative to the limit. Staying under about 15-20% utilization is usually healthier for a score, while balances over about 50% often start hurting more.
  • Avoid new monthly payments if buying is a real short-term goal.
  • Go through your monthly expenses and subscriptions. Car insurance, phone bills, streaming, memberships, and other recurring charges are all worth revisiting.
  • Get clear on the full monthly housing payment that feels comfortable in your life: principal, interest, taxes, insurance, mortgage insurance when needed, and HOA dues if the property has them.
  • Leave breathing room for maintenance, repairs, and the normal surprises that come with owning instead of renting.

Step 9

See how assistance may support the plan you are building.

These programs can help with down payment, closing costs, or mortgage structure, but they still work best when the payment, debt, and cash side already make sense. Think of assistance as one part of the preapproval conversation, not the whole conversation.

RI Housing

RI Housing has a few different first-time-buyer paths, and the right one usually depends on your credit, income, cash-to-close needs, and whether you qualify for a targeted program.

  • Extra Assistance: down payment and closing-cost help, often up to 6% of the price or $20,000, paired with a RIHousing first mortgage.
  • 15kDPA: $15,000 in zero-percent assistance for down payment and/or closing costs.
  • FirstGenHomeRI: $25,000 in down payment and/or closing-cost help for qualifying first-generation buyers.
  • RI REALTORS Homebuyer Grant: limited-time grant help that may pair with RIHousing assistance paths when available.
  • Homebuyer education is often part of the process.

MA Housing

MassHousing is more of a mortgage-and-benefits platform than a single assistance program. Buyers in Massachusetts may be looking at a MassHousing mortgage paired with other benefits built around it.

  • MassHousing Down Payment Assistance: up to $30,000, depending on eligibility and lender determination.
  • MassHousing mortgage loans: affordable mortgage options meant to pair with MassHousing benefits.
  • MIPlus: job-loss mortgage payment protection on MassHousing-insured loans at no added cost.
  • Veteran and service-member closing credit: a $2,500 closing-cost credit for eligible military households.
  • Program details vary by income, property type, approved lender, and exact loan structure.

What to do next

  • Match the housing agency to the state where you are actually buying: RI Housing for Rhode Island, MassHousing for Massachusetts.
  • Then narrow the question to which program path fits your starting point, budget, and cash-to-close needs.
  • Remember that assistance can help, but it does not replace qualifying for the payment.
  • Carry this into the next step so you know what cash you may need before preapproval, before shopping, and later once you are under contract.

Step 10

Know how your cash plan fits into preapproval and shopping.

Cash questions do not start at closing. Before a lender can give you a strong preapproval, it helps to know which funds are available for down payment, closing costs, reserves, gift funds, and the early out-of-pocket items that may show up once you go under contract.

Separate the cash buckets

Try to think about your funds in layers: down payment, closing costs, reserves, gift funds, and the smaller liquid items that may show up before closing.

Keep some money liquid

Many buyers feel more stable with roughly $3,000-$5,000 accessible for inspection, appraisal, and earnest-money timing later on. Those costs usually come after an offer is accepted, but they are easier to handle when you have planned for them before you start shopping.

Make the paper trail easy

Bank statements, seasoned funds, gift letters, and large transfers can all matter during preapproval. The clearer your money story is on paper, the easier the lender conversation usually becomes.

What to do next

  • List which funds are truly available now for down payment, closing costs, reserves, and gift money if that is part of the plan.
  • Try to keep roughly $3,000-$5,000 liquid if you can, so an inspection, an appraisal, and deposit timing feel manageable later.
  • Ask the lender which funds matter for qualifying, which funds matter before an offer, and which funds are due closer to closing.
  • If gift funds or assistance are part of the plan, ask how they need to be documented before you rely on them.

Step 11

Get your file ready for a real preapproval.

Once you know your credit picture, payment range, assistance paths, and cash plan, this is where it all comes together. Preapproval is the point where a lender starts verifying whether the loan, payment, cash, and overall file work on paper.

What the lender is really checking

  • Your income is stable and documented enough to support the payment.
  • Your monthly debts still leave enough room for the loan and the rest of your life.
  • You have real funds for down payment, closing costs, reserves, or gift money if that is part of the plan.
  • The loan program fits your credit, property type, and overall file.

What to gather now

  • Recent pay stubs, W-2s, and when needed tax returns.
  • Recent bank statements and any asset statements tied to your funds to close.
  • Photo ID plus employment, address, and housing history.
  • Documentation for bonuses, overtime, commission, self-employment, or gift funds if the lender asks.

What preapproval unlocks next

A real budget, a stronger offer position, and a clearer path into search, showings, and eventually writing the right offer.

What can slow the process down

Missing pages, unexplained transfers, brand-new debt, job changes, or not knowing exactly where the cash to close is coming from can all create extra lender questions.

Questions worth asking

Ask what payment feels comfortable, what cash is needed before the offer versus at closing, and which loan paths look realistic for your file.

What to do next

  • Pull your documents together before you need them so preapproval feels organized instead of rushed.
  • Get clear on which funds are truly available for down payment, closing costs, reserves, or gift funds.
  • Try not to open new credit right before preapproval.
  • Try not to move money around without being able to explain it if a lender asks.
  • Write down the questions you want answered before you apply so the lender conversation stays focused.
  • Once preapproved, the next phase is learning how search, showings, and offer writing usually flow.

Step 12

See how the process usually moves from preapproval to offer.

Once preapproval is in place, the process usually moves from search to showings to writing an offer. This is the brief version of how buyers move from planning mode into contract mode.

1. Preapproval in hand

You now know the budget, loan paths, and payment range you are using as your real guardrails.

2. Search + showings

You narrow the search to homes that fit real life, not just a max approval number or what looks good online.

3. Compare the real fit

You look past photos and price to taxes, condition, layout, payment, commute, and how the home actually supports your life.

4. Offer strategy

You decide what price, contingencies, timeline, and earnest-money amount make sense for the home and the market.

5. Offer submitted

Your agent sends the offer, the seller reviews it, and there may be a counter before everyone agrees on the same terms.

6. Offer accepted

Once both sides sign, the deal moves into under-contract mode and the inspection, appraisal, deposit, and lender timeline all matter on a real calendar.

What to notice about this part of the process

  • Preapproval usually happens before serious shopping, not after you find the perfect house online.
  • The search should stay anchored to payment, cash plan, and property fit, not just the biggest number a lender might approve.
  • Once an offer is accepted, the pace usually picks up quickly and the contract-to-close checkpoints begin.
  • The next step will unpack what inspections, appraisal, underwriting, and closing actually mean once you are under contract.

Step 13

Know what usually happens once you are under contract.

Once a seller signs, the process shifts into contract-to-close mode. This is where earnest money, inspections, appraisal, underwriting, walkthrough, and closing all start to matter on a real calendar.

Inspection

The inspection is your chance to understand the home's condition before you move deeper into the deal.

  • You choose the inspector and can usually attend.
  • The goal is to understand condition, deferred maintenance, systems, and risk.
  • It can lead to repairs, a credit, a renegotiation, peace of mind, or in the right inspection-contingency window, a termination where the buyer may get the deposit back.

Appraisal

The appraisal is the lender's value check, not your condition inspection.

  • The bank orders it to confirm the home supports the price and loan amount.
  • You usually pay for it up front, and it is handled on the lender side rather than as a buyer-attended appointment.
  • If value comes in low, the deal may need a renegotiation or financing change.

Mortgage commitment + clear to close

These are lender milestones that tell you financing is lining up the way it should.

  • Commitment means the lender is close, with final conditions or cleanup still possible.
  • Clear to close means underwriting is done and the loan is ready for closing.
  • This is the time to avoid new debt, big purchases, and large unexplained transfers between accounts.

Final walkthrough + closing

The walkthrough is your final condition check. Closing is the signing and transfer of ownership.

  • The walkthrough is not another inspection. It is a last look before funds and signatures move.
  • Closing is where the attorney or title side, lender, and final money all come together.
  • Once recording happens, the home is officially yours.

Contract-to-close version of what happens next

  • Earnest money is delivered, the contract calendar starts, and inspections usually get booked fast.
  • Inspection reports come back, and you decide whether to move forward as-is, ask for repairs, ask for a credit, or change course.
  • The lender and attorney or title side keep moving while the appraisal and underwriting milestones get checked off.
  • Once the lender issues commitment and then clear to close, you confirm final funds, do the walkthrough, and head to the closing table.
  • After recording, ownership transfers and the home is officially yours.

What to do next

  • Use this page to understand both sides of the journey: how to get ready for preapproval and what the process will ask of you once you are in it.
  • Reach out if you want help figuring out whether your next move is payment planning, preapproval, search, or just a little more preparation first.
  • Remember that being ready usually looks like understanding the next checkpoint, not having every future step solved all at once.
